# Outsiders — A Dreamtides Archetype Deep Dive

Outsiders is the tempo-control archetype of the Dreamtides pool. You might
expect a deck named for the Outsider subtype to be about the Outsider creatures
themselves, but it is really a shell that takes maximum advantage of the cheap
card selection and prevents that run through this pool. The Outsiders are the
visible payoff; the interaction is the engine. So that is where we will start.

## The interaction base

- Toll of Passage
- Defiant Parry
- Echoing Denial

First, the prevents. Because this pool is so heavily synergy-based, a timely
prevent on a key enabler or payoff sets the opponent back several turns.
Preventing a Goblin-Bombardment-style abandon outlet in an Abandon deck, for
instance, can leave them with a full board ready to die and no way to cash it
in. The low-cost prevents matter most because they cost so little ● that they
leave the rest of your turn free to advance your own board (by playing
Outsiders). Toll of Passage prevents a played card with cost 2● or less, and in
a pool whose curve tops out low it answers a large share of what the opponent
will cast. Defiant Parry and Echoing Denial both carry Offering, so on a turn
when you must keep ● open for your own plays you can banish a card from hand to
fire them for 0● — Defiant Parry as a soft tax (prevent unless the opponent pays
1●) and Echoing Denial as a hard prevent. Infernal Rest and Ripple of Defiance
round out the suite: Infernal Rest is the cheap tax, Ripple of Defiance the
Offering-backed hard prevent for events specifically.

Two prevents earn their slots by replacing what they stop with your own
advantage. Cragfall prevents a played card and then puts that card into your
hand — a clean two-for-one that converts the opponent's threat into your card.
Break the Sequence prevents a played card, returns it to the opponent's hand,
and draws you a card; it buys a full turn of tempo against an expensive play
while keeping your own hand stocked. Both reward the patient tempo line the deck
wants to play.

- Lurking Dread
- Dreaming Groves
- Pilgrim of Old Stones

A natural pairing for prevents is hand disruption — seeing the opponent's hand
tells you when to hold ● open and when to push your own board, and you get to
strip their best card while you are there. Lurking Dread discards a chosen card
with cost 2● or less from the opponent's hand; with the curve this low it hits a
large fraction of their deck. Dreaming Groves takes a chosen event specifically,
which is the line you want against a prevent-heavy or removal-heavy opponent:
pull the answer before you commit your threat. Pilgrim of Old Stones puts the
same effect on a 2● body — ▸Materialized: discard a chosen card from their hand
(they draw a card) — so it both disrupts and joins the board, and as a 2✦
character it can later be the ally you bounce to re-fire the discard.

- Molten Duel
- Grounded
- Standoff

Last, removal. Molten Duel dissolves a character with 3✦ or less for 1●,
clearing a blocker out of a lane so your threat can score. Grounded banishes an
enemy with cost exactly X●, scaling to whatever they have committed. Standoff
costs 0● but charges 5⍟ to dissolve any enemy — a steep price that the
grind-them-out plan can sometimes afford once you are ahead. Veil Shatter, an
Offering banish for an enemy with cost 3● or less, and Glimmerwood Scout, a body
that banishes on entry, extend the removal suite further (see Enablers).

The point of all this: the Outsiders deck is a great medium for cheap
interaction. The interaction is what makes the deck and the Outsiders are
secondary. This shell plays far more tempo-control than combo. The bounce loop
is the fun part, but it does nothing if the opponent dissolves your challenger
or races you out before you stabilize. You can build a fine tempo deck on the
prevent-and-disrupt base without a single Outsider; you cannot build a good
Outsiders deck without that base. There is heavy competition for the prevents,
disruption, and removal across every archetype, but much less for the Outsiders
and their enablers, so prioritize the interaction and let the payoffs come to
you.

## The bounce ability

The mechanic the archetype is named for is a single line of text shared across
the Outsider payoffs:

> ▸Materialized: Return another ally to hand. Move this character to that ally's
> position.

Read it against the rules and the tempo loop falls out. When the Outsider
materializes, it bounces a different ally to your hand and slides into that
ally's board position. Two things happen at once. First, the returned ally is a
card in your hand again — if it carried a ▸Materialized trigger, you get to fire
that trigger again the next time you play it, so the bounce is a way to re-buy
enter-play value turn after turn. Second, because the Outsider takes the bounced
ally's position, you can move a fresh challenger into the front rank in a single
play, then keep cycling that slot.

The cleanest version of the loop pairs the bounce with the good ▸Materialized
bodies covered below: play a value Outsider, bounce a Glimmerwood Scout or
Voidcaller back to hand, replay it next turn to banish another enemy, then bounce
it again. Every Outsider you draw becomes another rebuy. And because the bounce
returns "another ally" of any kind, a low-spark filler body that has already done
its job is a fine thing to pick up and recast.

## Enablers

- Glimmerwood Scout
- Voidcaller
- Cosmic Puppeteer

There are two kinds of enabler for Outsiders. The first are the high-value
▸Materialized bodies, like the three above. With these the bounce is as much
about re-buying their entry effect as it is about advancing a challenger.
Glimmerwood Scout banishes an enemy with cost 4● or less when it enters (it does
hand the opponent a card when it leaves play, so time the bounce); Voidcaller
banishes an enemy warrior on entry, a sharp answer to Warrior Aggro; Cosmic
Puppeteer gains control of an enemy with cost X● or less, stealing a threat and
swelling your board at once. Bounce any of them, replay, and the effect fires
again. This combination is backbreaking — you get the value of the Outsider's
own scoring trigger plus a re-bought removal or theft each cycle. Decks built
this way lean controlling, since the bodies themselves are reasonable win
conditions.

- Duskmount
- Keeper of the Tides
- Dreadwood Emissary

You can lean further into the rematerialize angle with a small blink package.
Duskmount enters Awakened and reads "☪: Rematerialize an ally. This character
gains +1✦," firing a chosen ally's ▸Materialized again every turn while it grows
— point it at Glimmerwood Scout or Voidcaller for a recurring banish without even
spending a card. Keeper of the Tides is a disruptive body in this slot:
▸Materialized prevents a played card with cost 2● or less, and it carries the
"❖ – 1●: Move this character to an unoccupied character position" repositioning
ability so it can slide forward to challenge. Dreadwood Emissary gives an event
in your void reclaim until end of turn, re-buying a spent prevent or removal
event — exactly the interaction this deck wants more of. Interaction stays vital
in this version, but the disruptive bodies double as that interaction.

- Signal Resonant
- Saffron Trailblazer
- Planetgazer

The second kind of enabler is the aggressive one. These decks have a different
plan: stick a threat early and defend it. Several cheap bodies carry the
repositioning ability "❖ – 1●: Move this character to an unoccupied character
position," which is the deck's evasion analog — for 1● during a Fast window you
slide a character into an open front-rank lane where no defender sits opposite,
so it goes unpaired and scores its spark as ⍟. Signal Resonant is the standout:
a 1● body that has all character types, so it counts as an Outsider for the
payoff triggers and can be the ally a bounce returns and recasts for double
value. Saffron Trailblazer enters Awakened, so it can reposition and challenge
the turn it lands. Planetgazer adds a ▸Materialized Foresee 2 to smooth your
draws while it threatens a lane. Turn-1 Signal Resonant into turn-2 Driftrider
gets the scoring engine online fast, and because Signal Resonant is itself an
Outsider, every time it scores it counts toward the "when an allied outsider
scores ⍟" payoffs.

In these aggressive builds the cheap prevents are essential — you are trying to
establish a lead, and keeping your early challenger alive and its lane clear is
the whole game. Tempo plays like Ridgecutter ("Return a character to hand. Gain
2●") are extra valuable here: bounce an enemy blocker out of a lane and net ●
while you do it, setting the opponent back while your challenger connects.

## Payoffs

- Vanishing Inquisitor
- Northlight Maestro
- Driftrider

The Outsiders themselves are all built the same way: each carries the bounce
ability and draws cards when it scores ⍟. Vanishing Inquisitor is the cheap one
at 1● — bounce on entry, and "when this character scores ⍟, draw a card, then
discard a card" to filter while it grinds. Northlight Maestro at 2● draws cleanly
when it scores. The two scoring triggers above key only off the body itself,
while Driftrider widens the payoff: "when an allied outsider scores ⍟, foresee 1,
then draw a card," so it fires off any Outsider on the board, including a
front-line Signal Resonant. With several Outsiders down, the draws stack and the
board gets out of hand fast. Driftrider is the best of the three for the scaling
reason, but the 1● Vanishing Inquisitor is excellent precisely because it is so
cheap to deploy and bounce.

- Thronebound Arbiter
- Last Beacon

Thronebound Arbiter is the top-end Outsider at 4●: it bounces on entry and draws
a card whenever any allied outsider scores, the same board-wide trigger as
Driftrider stapled to a sturdier 2✦ payoff. Last Beacon is not an Outsider
itself, but it fits perfectly. It makes Outsider figments ("3●, ☪: Materialize a
1✦ outsider figment"), giving you extra scoring bodies that trigger the
outsider-scores payoffs, and its own trigger — "when an allied outsider scores ⍟,
draw a card and gain ⍟ equal to its ● cost" — turns each scoring Outsider into
cards and bonus points. The figments it makes are Outsiders too, so they feed
Driftrider and Thronebound Arbiter as well; stacking these payoffs is how the
deck pulls away. Last Beacon plays a strong supporting role in any tempo list
that plans to score with cheap evasive bodies, even without a full Outsider
package.

- Dreadcall Warden
- Oracle of Shifting Skies

A couple of card-advantage engines protect the grind. Dreadcall Warden reads
"▸Night: Draw a card, then discard a card unless you have an allied challenger,"
so as long as you have committed a challenger — which the deck wants to do anyway
— it is a clean draw each turn. Oracle of Shifting Skies smooths an event-heavy
hand: "when you play an event, foresee 1," which rewards the dense prevent-and-
removal base by letting you sculpt your draws every time you interact.

## Winning the game

- Vanishing Inquisitor
- Thronebound Arbiter
- Last Beacon

Winning happens more as a side effect of grinding the opponent out than from any
dedicated finisher. Disrupt them with prevents and hand attack until they have
nothing left, then close with unpaired Outsiders scoring spark in open lanes. The
repositioning bodies do the actual scoring: slide Signal Resonant or Saffron
Trailblazer into an empty front-rank lane and it converts its spark to ⍟, while
the scoring triggers on Vanishing Inquisitor, Northlight Maestro, Driftrider, and
Thronebound Arbiter refill your hand so the disruption never runs dry. Last
Beacon adds direct points on top, gaining ⍟ equal to the ● cost of each Outsider
that scores.

It pays to track how many threats you have left in your deck so you know how hard
to fight for the bodies already in play. You can win on the back of a single
recurring Outsider — bounce it, replay it, and keep sliding it into open lanes —
but that game has to be played carefully, and recognizing when one challenger is
your only route to victory can decide the match. You can also fall into races
where the cheap repositioning challengers matter most: a 2✦ body scoring in an
unpaired lane every turn closes faster than it looks, especially while you are
drawing extra cards off the scoring triggers each turn.

## Overlap with other archetypes

Outsiders shares its disruptive base with Events, which also wants a high density
of cheap prevents and removal; the difference is the win condition, with Events
leaning on event-payoff characters rather than the bounce loop. The
rematerialize line through Duskmount overlaps with Blink, and the ▸Materialized
value bodies (Glimmerwood Scout, Voidcaller, Cosmic Puppeteer) are at home in
both. Signal Resonant and Sky Voyager, which have all character types, splash
into any tribal shell. Against Warrior Aggro, Voidcaller's warrior banish and the
cheap prevents give Outsiders a strong disruptive angle, which is much of why the
tempo-control plan holds up across the format.
